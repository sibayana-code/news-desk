Status

Where things stand

Flights: The nationwide ground stop ended at 8:15 p.m. Eastern Friday (9:15 a.m. Saturday PH) after more than four hours, and the airspace is open. Knock-on disruption ran through Saturday: FlightAware’s final tally for the day was 6,620 cancellations and 8,316 delays within, into or out of the United States, after about 9,200 cancellations on Friday (FlightAware; the FAA’s own count of Friday cancellations by all operators is about 12,462). Delta, United and American each cancelled about a quarter of Saturday’s flights and JetBlue nearly 45% (Reuters). Sunday closed with more than 2,300 US cancellations and about 7,700–8,000 delays (FlightAware via NPR and CNBC), with Atlanta and Dallas-Fort Worth worst hit; roughly 18,000 cancellations over three days. Monday’s board showed 144 cancellations on Sunday afternoon, and Duffy expects full capacity on Monday Eastern.

Cause: At a Saturday news conference, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said contractor L3Harris, which owns and runs the FAA’s telecom network, was switching lines from old copper to fiber at “at least one” remote FAA site when a router began flooding other routers with more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, disrupting radio contact with aircraft and radar feeds. The Wall Street Journal says the traffic came from newly installed routers at an FAA radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas (a Juniper unit) and a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, and the overload hit 17 of the 22 en route centers. A router broadcasting feature has been switched off on the FAA’s roughly 60 Juniper routers; HPE says the router “functioned as it was configured to by the installer.” Both routers are now in L3Harris’s Florida lab for diagnostics with FAA, Juniper and Cisco engineers, and Duffy says broadcasting is off on all 69 such routers. Whether it was installer error or a faulty router is not yet determined, and Bedford said the root cause has not been established. Bedford said there is “zero evidence of anything nefarious, and zero evidence of anything AI related”; Duffy said Sunday there is “no indication” of a foreign cyberattack and the FBI has offered help, but officials have not formally closed the cyber question.

Who’s investigating: The FAA, with L3Harris, which says it is supporting the inquiry and has finished more than 70% of the copper-to-fiber conversion. The PASS union, which represents FAA technicians, says its members were not involved in the upgrade. DHS, CISA, NSA, the Pentagon and the FBI took part in Friday’s response. Rep. Robert Garcia, the top Democrat on House Oversight, has pledged his own investigation. Duffy says he cannot cancel L3Harris because it owns the network. Next: the FAA’s extra testing with L3Harris for further risks, a finding on the faulty router versus contractor error, a published root-cause analysis, which Bedford promised to share, a review of when upgrade work at remote sites is scheduled, Duffy’s $50 billion funding request (House Speaker Mike Johnson says he supports more money this year), and oversight from House Democrats Rick Larsen and Andre Carson, who take over the transport committee and aviation subcommittee next year: a staff briefing on Monday 16 Nov Eastern and a hearing with Bedford expected in early December.

Unconfirmed: theories and analysis

Each item is attributed to a named outlet or expert. None of these is an official finding.

The trouble was limited to Cisco 600-series routers made in 2001–2006

Senior government sources told CBS News the FAA’s preliminary probe points to Cisco 600-series routers manufactured between 2001 and 2006, and that the faulty equipment has been identified and isolated. Bedford separately said the FAA routinely replaces about 2,000 Cisco 600 routers with Juniper ones.

Why it might be true: It fits Bedford’s description of an ageing router fleet being swapped out, and Guy’s account of specific devices flooding the network. Why it might be false: The FAA has not named a model on the record, CBS’s own sources reported conflicting accounts, and Duffy’s version centres on a router being installed in the copper-to-fiber work, which may be new equipment rather than the old Cisco units.

CBS News · DOT transcript

A software update on the L3Harris-run FAA telecom network caused a reboot and crash

Multiple sources told CBS News that an update to the private communications network connecting the air-traffic system caused it to reboot, then crash, producing the outage. The system is operated by L3Harris, which had not commented when CBS, Scientific American and The Register asked. The Register notes that the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure, managed by L3Harris, links more than 4,400 facilities. Bloomberg, citing a person familiar with the matter, said the FBI is examining whether a software update caused the outage.

Why it might be true: It fits Duffy’s later account of overloaded routers and switches, and The Air Current’s reporting that the failure began at one center and cascaded. Why it might be false: The FAA has not confirmed a software update or named a contractor. L3Harris has not commented, and the root cause is still under investigation. On Saturday Duffy instead described a router that flooded the network during L3Harris’s copper-to-fiber work, and said it is undetermined whether installer error or a faulty router was to blame, which is not the same as a software update.

The new SMART AI traffic tool, expanded on 10 November, played a role

Mullin said officials were “looking at” any possible relationship to SMART, the FAA’s Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories system, which Duffy said was activated at all 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers on 10 November. Congressional Democrats had already asked for a pause after the first rollout, citing transparency and safety concerns. Air Space Intelligence, which built SMART, said: “The current ground stop is not based on or a result of the SMART system, any product of ASI or our integration with FAA and ATC.”

Why it might be true: The nationwide expansion was only three days old, and the DHS secretary said it was part of the inquiry. Why it might be false: The vendor denies any role, Duffy said there was “no possible way” SMART was involved because it is not connected to the core flight-tracking systems (GeekWire), and his briefing pointed at analog switches and routers on the telecom network, not a traffic-flow recommendation tool.

A Voice Switching and Control System update went wrong

Ruth Stilwell, a former air traffic controller and aerospace policy expert, told Scientific American the outage may have been a software problem, “such as a software update gone awry in the Voice Switching and Control System, which manages communications between pilots and air traffic controllers.” She said it would be unusual to push an update during the day, but that the FAA is short-staffed. She also said the problem was not Microsoft software.

Why it might be true: A voice-switch failure would explain lost radio contact between controllers and pilots, which is what centers reported. Why it might be false: Stilwell presented this as speculation. Officials have talked about routers and switches in general and have not named VSCS.

Scientific American

A cyberattack or criminal sabotage

Because this was only the third nationwide ground stop, after September 11, 2001 and the 2023 NOTAM failure, a hostile act was an early question. Mullin said there was no indication of a “nefarious attack,” that the public should not be afraid, and later that the problem appears mechanically driven, while refusing to rule a cyber cause out entirely. A person familiar with the matter told Bloomberg the FBI does not believe the communications failure was a cyberattack or a crime.

Why it might be true: A near-simultaneous failure across many centers is the sort of event investigators would test for an attack, and officials have not closed that door. Why it might be false: DHS, the FBI source and Duffy’s mechanical explanation all currently point the other way, and Mullin said no one had claimed responsibility. On Saturday FAA Administrator Bedford said there is “zero evidence of anything nefarious,” and Duffy traced the failure to a router during contractor maintenance (CNBC, BBC).

ABC News live updates · Bloomberg, via Moneycontrol

A link to the global Microsoft 365 outage

A major Microsoft 365 disruption, especially to email, ran from Thursday into Friday and prompted questions about a connection. Mullin said the ground stop was not connected to that outage, MyNorthwest reported. The New York Times, cited by MyNorthwest, reported that Microsoft said it had no indication the ground stop was related. The Register likewise found no present indication the two incidents were related. Stilwell told Scientific American the FAA problem was not Microsoft software.

Why it might be true: The two failures overlapped, which made a shared-infrastructure theory reasonable to test. Why it might be false: Both Mullin and Microsoft have now said they see no link, and the FAA’s own description has been of its telecom and radar network, not office software.
