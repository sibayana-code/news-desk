Status

Where things stand

Flights: The nationwide ground stop ended at 8:15 p.m. Eastern Friday (9:15 a.m. Saturday PH) after more than four hours, and the airspace is open. Knock-on disruption ran through Saturday: FlightAware’s final tally for the day was 6,620 cancellations and 8,316 delays within, into or out of the United States, after about 9,200 cancellations on Friday (FlightAware; the FAA’s own count of Friday cancellations by all operators is about 12,462). Delta, United and American each cancelled about a quarter of Saturday’s flights and JetBlue nearly 45% (Reuters). Sunday closed with more than 2,300 US cancellations and about 7,700–8,000 delays (FlightAware via NPR and CNBC), with Atlanta and Dallas-Fort Worth worst hit; roughly 18,000 cancellations over three days. Monday stayed near normal: by about 6:20 p.m. Eastern (7:20 a.m. Tuesday PH), FlightAware listed 349 US cancellations and 5,158 delays for the day. Wednesday was close to normal, with 48 US cancellations and 707 delays by 9:50 a.m. Eastern (FlightAware via NJ.com).

Cause: At a Saturday news conference, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said contractor L3Harris, which owns and runs the FAA’s telecom network, was switching lines from old copper to fiber at “at least one” remote FAA site when a router began flooding other routers with more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, disrupting radio contact with aircraft and radar feeds. The Wall Street Journal says the traffic came from newly installed routers at an FAA radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas (a Juniper unit) and a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, and the overload hit 17 of the 22 en route centers. A router broadcasting feature has been switched off on the FAA’s roughly 60 Juniper routers; HPE says the router “functioned as it was configured to by the installer.” Both routers are now in L3Harris’s Florida lab for diagnostics with FAA, Juniper and Cisco engineers and FBI agents (Reuters says the FBI has not taken possession of any equipment), who are also reviewing how the router was installed; the Washington Post reports an FAA official supervised and signed off on the Arkansas work eight minutes before the network faltered, and Duffy says broadcasting is off on all 69 such routers. Duffy says the new routers stayed up and it was the FAA’s old 2001-era Cisco units that were flooded. Engineers in the Florida lab have reproduced the flood but not yet found why it happened (CNBC), and CBS reports L3Harris’s network operations center took hours to find the source, which delayed lifting the stop. Whether it was installer error or a faulty router is not yet determined, and Bedford said the root cause has not been established. Bedford said there is “zero evidence of anything nefarious, and zero evidence of anything AI related”; Duffy said Sunday there is “no indication” of a foreign cyberattack and the FBI has offered help, but officials have not formally closed the cyber question. On Tuesday (Eastern) DNI Jay Clayton said the week’s outages, the FAA’s included, were “not a cyberattack” or nation-state effort.

Who’s investigating: The FAA, with L3Harris, which says it is supporting the inquiry and has finished more than 70% of the copper-to-fiber conversion. The PASS union, which represents FAA technicians, says its members were not involved in the upgrade. DHS, CISA, NSA, the Pentagon and the FBI took part in Friday’s response. Rep. Robert Garcia, the top Democrat on House Oversight, has pledged his own investigation. Duffy says he cannot cancel L3Harris because it owns the network. Next: a public briefing by Duffy on the root cause, which Bedford says will come this week (Eastern), the FAA’s extra testing with L3Harris for further risks, a finding on the faulty router versus contractor error, a published root-cause analysis, which Bedford promised to share, a review of when upgrade work at remote sites is scheduled, Duffy’s $50 billion funding request (Bedford concedes “whiplash” after a $30 billion letter went up days earlier; House Speaker Mike Johnson says he supports more money this year, but Senate Republicans including John Thune and John Cornyn want an accounting of the $12.5 billion first), a skeptical Senate GOP after Duffy’s Tuesday lunch briefing (Commerce Chair Ted Cruz says the full $50 billion won’t be funded) (the formal $50.1 billion request is now with Congress, tied to the 11 Dec funding deadline), the FAA’s pause on further Juniper router installs (400 of a planned 3,648 installed, 69 operational), which 13 Senate Commerce Democrats want kept until independent testing, their requested FAA–L3Harris–Juniper technical briefing by 23 Nov, and oversight from House Democrats Rick Larsen and Andre Carson, who take over the transport committee and aviation subcommittee next year: a staff briefing on Monday 16 Nov Eastern and a hearing with Bedford expected in early December.

  1. Fri 20 Nov, about 10:20 AM PH TechTarget: the quarantined Juniper units were circuit-to-packet devices, and their broadcast mode is off by default

    A source familiar with the issue told TechTarget the devices the FAA cut off to steady the network were circuit-to-packet (CTP) units that bridge legacy analog lines to IP, not standard routers, and that their broadcast (multicast) feature is not on by default; turning it on takes configuration code, not a simple toggle, and other sites were set up the same way without trouble. HPE repeated that its equipment “was working as configured.” As of Thursday night Eastern, the government had not yet announced the root cause that Bedford said would come this week.

Unconfirmed: theories and analysis

Each item is attributed to a named outlet or expert. None of these is an official finding.

Unconfirmed: a misconfiguration or poor network design, not the vendor’s hardware, is to blame

Speaking to TechTarget, Wipro network architect Ram Chandra Sachan (in a personal capacity) said a single device rarely takes down a network this widely because key entry points are normally redundant, and called it “misconfiguration or poorly designed network architecture,” not an OEM problem. Red Hat engineer Michele Chubirka, stressing she was speculating without a postmortem, said a “faulty” Juniper router is rare and bad configs or loops from mis-plugged hardware are common. EMA analyst Shamus McGillicuddy said he doubts it is HPE or Juniper’s fault.

Why it might be true: The broadcast feature had to be deliberately configured, HPE says the unit behaved as configured, and Duffy says 68 earlier installs worked. Why it might be false: No postmortem has been published, the lab has reproduced the flood without explaining it, and the Amarillo unit installed weeks earlier also flooded, so a device or software fault is not ruled out.

TechTarget

Unconfirmed: four big outages in under a week “look suspiciously” like a cyberattack

Mike Wiacek, CTO of Stairwell and founder of Google’s Threat Analysis Group, told Network World he “can’t say for certain” either way, but that the FAA, Outlook, bank and NSA outages together look like what he would expect an attack to look like, and that officials ruled one out too fast: “You can’t compress months of forensic work into a few hours.” In the same piece, NetBox Labs CEO Kris Beevers said the FAA network is not generally reachable from outside and that config changes often look like an internal denial-of-service.

Why it might be true: The timing is unusual, and the forensic work on the routers is still under way in L3Harris’s lab. Why it might be false: The FAA has traced the flood to specific newly installed hardware at two sites, the DNI says intelligence agencies see no sign of coordination, and the other companies have each named their own internal causes.

Network World

The Arkansas device isn’t really a router, and its flood may not be the root cause

A source close to the incident told CRN that no one has determined the HPE Juniper device malfunctioned or that its flooding was the root cause, and that the unit is technically a copper-to-fiber conversion device with a multicast feature rather than a router. The source added that more than 60 similar devices were deployed without incident.

Why it might be true: It fits HPE’s line that its gear worked “as configured” and the FAA’s own statement that the root cause is not yet determined. Why it might be false: The source is anonymous and may speak for the vendor side; the FAA, DOT and Duffy have all described the El Dorado unit as a router that flooded the network.

CRN

The Juniper router’s broadcasting feature should never have been switched on next to legacy Cisco gear

A source with knowledge of the incident told CRN that the broadcasting capability on the new HPE Juniper router should have been turned off because it was talking to 20-year-old Cisco routers: “That capability should not have been turned on for a large legacy system.”

Why it might be true: It matches the FAA’s fix (disabling broadcasting on all the new routers stopped the flood), HPE’s line that its equipment worked “as configured”, and Duffy’s account that the old Cisco units were the ones overwhelmed. Why it might be false: The source is anonymous, the lab diagnostics are not finished, and officials still list a faulty router as a possibility alongside installer error.

CRN

The trouble was limited to Cisco 600-series routers made in 2001–2006

Senior government sources told CBS News the FAA’s preliminary probe points to Cisco 600-series routers manufactured between 2001 and 2006, and that the faulty equipment has been identified and isolated. Bedford separately said the FAA routinely replaces about 2,000 Cisco 600 routers with Juniper ones.

Why it might be true: It fits Bedford’s description of an ageing router fleet being swapped out, and Guy’s account of specific devices flooding the network. Why it might be false: The FAA has not named a model on the record, CBS’s own sources reported conflicting accounts, and Duffy’s version centres on a router being installed in the copper-to-fiber work, which may be new equipment rather than the old Cisco units.

CBS News · DOT transcript

A software update on the L3Harris-run FAA telecom network caused a reboot and crash

Multiple sources told CBS News that an update to the private communications network connecting the air-traffic system caused it to reboot, then crash, producing the outage. The system is operated by L3Harris, which had not commented when CBS, Scientific American and The Register asked. The Register notes that the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure, managed by L3Harris, links more than 4,400 facilities. Bloomberg, citing a person familiar with the matter, said the FBI is examining whether a software update caused the outage.

Why it might be true: It fits Duffy’s later account of overloaded routers and switches, and The Air Current’s reporting that the failure began at one center and cascaded. Why it might be false: The FAA has not confirmed a software update or named a contractor. L3Harris has not commented, and the root cause is still under investigation. On Saturday Duffy instead described a router that flooded the network during L3Harris’s copper-to-fiber work, and said it is undetermined whether installer error or a faulty router was to blame, which is not the same as a software update.

CBS News, via AOL · The Register · Bloomberg, via Moneycontrol

The new SMART AI traffic tool, expanded on 10 November, played a role

Mullin said officials were “looking at” any possible relationship to SMART, the FAA’s Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories system, which Duffy said was activated at all 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers on 10 November. Congressional Democrats had already asked for a pause after the first rollout, citing transparency and safety concerns. Air Space Intelligence, which built SMART, said: “The current ground stop is not based on or a result of the SMART system, any product of ASI or our integration with FAA and ATC.”

Why it might be true: The nationwide expansion was only three days old, and the DHS secretary said it was part of the inquiry. Why it might be false: The vendor denies any role, Duffy said there was “no possible way” SMART was involved because it is not connected to the core flight-tracking systems (GeekWire), and his briefing pointed at analog switches and routers on the telecom network, not a traffic-flow recommendation tool.

The Air Current · Los Angeles Times · The Register · GeekWire

A Voice Switching and Control System update went wrong

Ruth Stilwell, a former air traffic controller and aerospace policy expert, told Scientific American the outage may have been a software problem, “such as a software update gone awry in the Voice Switching and Control System, which manages communications between pilots and air traffic controllers.” She said it would be unusual to push an update during the day, but that the FAA is short-staffed. She also said the problem was not Microsoft software.

Why it might be true: A voice-switch failure would explain lost radio contact between controllers and pilots, which is what centers reported. Why it might be false: Stilwell presented this as speculation. Officials have talked about routers and switches in general and have not named VSCS.

Scientific American

A cyberattack or criminal sabotage

Because this was only the third nationwide ground stop, after September 11, 2001 and the 2023 NOTAM failure, a hostile act was an early question. Mullin said there was no indication of a “nefarious attack,” that the public should not be afraid, and later that the problem appears mechanically driven, while refusing to rule a cyber cause out entirely. A person familiar with the matter told Bloomberg the FBI does not believe the communications failure was a cyberattack or a crime.

Why it might be true: A near-simultaneous failure across many centers is the sort of event investigators would test for an attack, and officials have not closed that door. Why it might be false: DHS, the FBI source and Duffy’s mechanical explanation all currently point the other way, and Mullin said no one had claimed responsibility. On Saturday FAA Administrator Bedford said there is “zero evidence of anything nefarious,” and Duffy traced the failure to a router during contractor maintenance (CNBC, BBC).

ABC News live updates · Bloomberg, via Moneycontrol

A link to the global Microsoft 365 outage

A major Microsoft 365 disruption, especially to email, ran from Thursday into Friday and prompted questions about a connection. Mullin said the ground stop was not connected to that outage, MyNorthwest reported. The New York Times, cited by MyNorthwest, reported that Microsoft said it had no indication the ground stop was related. The Register likewise found no present indication the two incidents were related. Stilwell told Scientific American the FAA problem was not Microsoft software.

Why it might be true: The two failures overlapped, which made a shared-infrastructure theory reasonable to test. Why it might be false: Both Mullin and Microsoft have now said they see no link, and the FAA’s own description has been of its telecom and radar network, not office software.
